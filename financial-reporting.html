Sweet Bamboo internship

Monthly P&L reporting

Giving managers a clearer view of revenue, expenses and profitability by store.

ToolsGoogle Sheets, Excel and Apps Script
WorkTransaction mapping and monthly reporting
FilesPresentation, report and workbook

Data note: Company financial figures have been replaced with example data for confidentiality. The example results are not the company’s actual results or measured improvements from the project.

Why I started

Managers knew roughly how much each restaurant earned and spent, but there was no monthly P&L to show where the money was going. That made it difficult to compare stores or explain why profitability changed.

What I built

I used transaction exports from the Sweet Bamboo app and bank records to organize revenue and expenses into consistent categories. I built the category mappings, the review process for unclear transactions and the monthly P&L formulas and layout. I also used Apps Script to reduce repetitive work when updating the report.

How I handled the data

A description alone was not always enough to classify a transaction. I separated entries that needed review instead of forcing them into a category. I also distinguished excluded items from operating expenses so the report’s totals had a clear basis.

The aim was a process that could be updated each month, with the same definitions used across stores.

What changed

The project introduced a categorized monthly view of store profitability where managers had previously relied on sales and spending totals.

That gave management a way to discuss food costs, labor and channel expenses separately. I have not yet measured a financial return or reporting-time reduction from this project.

What I would track next

  • Time from month-end to a reviewed report.
  • Transactions still waiting for classification.
  • Unexplained differences from source totals.
  • Whether agreed management actions were followed up.

Reading the monthly comparison

The sample presentation shows how revenue and expense changes flow through to the operating result.

What I looked for

I compared both total monthly sales and sales per day. A shorter month can explain part of a sales decline, so I did not want to treat every monthly change as weaker demand.

I then looked at expenses as a share of sales. A cost could fall in dollars but still take up more of revenue. Breaking the profit change into sales and expense movements helped show which items needed a closer review.

Assumptions behind the report

Food purchases measure spending, while food consumption also depends on opening and closing inventory. The month assigned to a transaction depends on its timing. Shared costs also need a consistent allocation rule before comparing stores.

What the report can tell a manager

It helps identify where to investigate. It does not, by itself, tell us whether a higher food cost came from purchase prices, portions, waste or stock timing.

Those questions need operational records or a conversation with the store manager.
