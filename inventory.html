Inventory and purchasing

Using sales reports to help managers plan how much to order.

ToolsMenuSifu reports, Google Sheets and Apps Script
Ordering cycleBiweekly
FilesPresentation and workbook

Data note: This public workbook uses example sales, recipes and stock records for confidentiality. It is a smaller reconstruction of the workflow used at Elmhurst.

The problem with ordering

Before placing an order, an employee had to go into the freezer and check what was left. Managers then estimated how much to buy for the next two weeks. This took time, and ordering depended heavily on judgment. We had both excess stock and items running out.

What I built

I used the daily transaction reports emailed by MenuSifu. An Apps Script located those emails and imported the sales data into Google Sheets.

I then mapped menu items to the ingredients needed to make them. That meant accounting for different sizes and meal combinations, then converting estimated usage into the units used when ordering from suppliers.

How the pieces connect

Daily dish quantities feed the ingredient calculations. The purchasing view compares expected demand with stock and incoming deliveries, then rounds the suggested order to supplier packs.

How managers used it

The workflow was put in place and used by Elmhurst managers to support purchasing.

It gave them a more consistent starting point for an order. They still needed to check stock and use their judgment when demand or deliveries changed.

What the downloadable file contains

A compact Excel example of the mappings, ingredient usage, pack conversions and order calculations. The live email connection and Apps Script triggers are described in the presentation.

Calculating the order

All quantities need to use the same supplier units before stock and deliveries can be deducted.

The assumptions behind it

The usage estimate assumes each dish uses the mapped ingredient portion. Actual usage can differ because of portioning, waste or a recipe change. A sales report also cannot tell us whether a delivery was received correctly.

For that reason, physical counts and receiving records still matter. A difference between expected stock and counted stock is something to investigate, rather than automatically overwrite.

What I would measure

I would compare order-preparation time and stockouts across several order cycles, while recording waste and stock-count differences. For the forecast itself, I would compare each saved forecast with usage for the same ingredient and period.

Checks before placing an order

  • Did every sales day import?
  • Are new menu items mapped?
  • Are pack sizes and portions current?
  • Have incoming deliveries been confirmed?
  • Will the order fit storage and shelf-life limits?
